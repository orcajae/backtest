Could timing like this come from chance? Stress test only

The luck check has two separate parts. Part (a) shows how uncertain the result itself is. Part (b) compares the rule with random timing that keeps the same shape.

(a) Uncertainty band

The rule’s daily returns resampled in 20-day blocks, 1,000 times. The middle 95% of resampled paths had a return a year from 1.6% to 10.8% and a worst fall from −49.1% to −19.4%. This describes uncertainty in the result; it is not a test of luck.

(b) Random timing with the same shape

The rule’s 93 stretches in the market and 92 stretches in cash were each put in a random order, keeping every stretch’s length, 1,000 times. Time in the market (74.4%), the number of trades (184), the cost per fill and the fill rule stay the same. Only when the stretches happen changes. This is not a shuffle of individual trades.

How to read it: p is the share of random-timing paths that did at least as well as the rule (with one added to each count). Compared with this random timing, this sample gives weak grounds to say the rule’s result lies outside what such timing produces. That is not proof that the rule has no edge, and it is not a pass or fail: no significance level was set before the run, the two measures were not corrected for being tested together, and the seed is fixed so a rerun gives the same p (that is reproducibility, not new evidence). The comparison holds only for this one kind of random timing.

Rising, falling and sideways markets Stress test only

Every measured day is put in one group by how SPY had moved over the previous 63 trading days (about three months), measured up to the previous close: up more than 5% is rising, down more than 5% is falling, anything in between (including exactly 5%) is sideways. The group is known before the day’s open, so no price from that day is used. Days without enough earlier closes would be shown as not classified.

Trading cost: 0.05% of traded value per side, deducted on each fill. Buy and hold is held from the close before the first measured day and pays no trading cost in this sample.

Definitions

  • Hold SPY while its adjusted close is strictly above its 200-day simple average of adjusted closes; otherwise hold cash. A close equal to the average counts as cash.
  • Decided on the adjusted close of trading day t. Filled at the open of the next trading session t+1, the next day the exchange is open (adjusted open for returns; unadjusted open shown as the fill price).
  • 0.05% of traded value per side, deducted on each fill. No other slippage. Cash earns 0%.
  • Equity is 1.0 at the close of 31 Dec 1999; the first measured day is 3 Jan 2000.
  • Sharpe ratio: mean daily return over its standard deviation, times √252, no risk-free rate.
  • Worst fall and time below a previous peak are measured on daily values.

Limits

  • Past prices only. Nothing here predicts future returns.
  • Fills use the opening price of the next trading session. A real order at the open can fill differently; slippage at the open is not measured, so these are not verified trading results.
  • Cash earns 0%. With interest on cash the rule would look somewhat better.
  • Taxes and borrowing are not modelled.
  • One market and one data source. A rule that works on SPY may not work elsewhere.
  • An earlier version of this sample filled each trade at the same close that produced its signal. That is an idealised assumption, not a test of whether such trades could be executed; this version fills at the next session’s open instead.
