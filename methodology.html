How a backtest is run

The same method for every report, written down before your rules are run.

1. Your rules, fixed first

Entry, exit, position size, market and dates are written out and confirmed with you before any result exists. If a rule is ambiguous, I ask; I never pick the reading that looks best.

2. Data

Stocks and ETFs
Daily prices adjusted for splits and dividends, from public sources such as Yahoo Finance.
Crypto
Public exchange price history for spot markets.
Your data
If you send your own data, it is used as given and named in the report.

3. Timing

A signal from a bar's close trades at the open of the next trading session (the next day the exchange is open), unless your rules name another time. Nothing in a calculation uses a price from after its own date. This one rule removes the most common reason a backtest looks better than reality.

4. Costs

Your broker's costs if you give them. Otherwise 0.05% a side for US stocks and ETFs and 0.10% a side for crypto spot, stated in the report. The report also shows how much the result changes if costs double.

5. What a quick backtest reports

Return a year, worst fall from a peak, Sharpe ratio, longest time below a previous peak, number of trades, time in the market, and the same figures for simply holding the market over the same dates.

6. What a stress test adds

Other settings
Every number in your rules moved up and down. A result that holds only at one value is reported as fragile.
Split periods
The dates cut in two or more parts, so one lucky stretch cannot carry the whole result.
Walk-forward
Settings chosen on earlier data, then tested on later data they never saw.
Luck check
Two parts. A resampling band shows how uncertain the result itself is. Then the rule’s stretches in the market and in cash are put in random order many times, keeping their lengths, the time in the market and the number of trades, to see how often random timing of the same shape does as well.
Market regimes
Results in rising, falling and sideways markets, shown separately. Each day is grouped using only prices known before it; the report states the definition.

7. What a report never says

It never tells you to trade, which settings to use, or what to buy. It describes how your rules behaved on past prices, and where that behaviour looks weak. Past results do not predict future returns.
